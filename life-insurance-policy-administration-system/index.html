Modernizing Life Insurance Policy Administration Systems for LDTI

Published October 13, 2026  ·  9 min read

A life insurer decides to move its term and whole life policies from a legacy administration platform to a modern system. Operations and IT lead the project, aiming to retire the old platform, improve servicing screens, and lower maintenance costs. The team focuses on mapping the data that service representatives need to do their jobs.

Three months before the planned cutover, the actuarial team runs its first LDTI calculations using the converted data. The results reveal several problems. Some policy issue dates have changed, historical premium records are incomplete, and certain riders use different codes in the new system. As a result, policies no longer fall into the same cohorts as they did in previous quarters. The finance team now faces a difficult choice: delay the conversion or risk having to revise previously reported disclosures.

This example highlights how LDTI has changed the requirements for policy administration projects. The life insurance policy administration system supplies data used to measure liabilities by cohort and prepare quarterly financial disclosures. Modernization projects must account for these reporting requirements from the beginning. Finance and actuarial teams need to share ownership of the project rather than review decisions after operations and IT have made them.

What LDTI Changed

The Financial Accounting Standards Board (FASB) issued Accounting Standards Update 2018-12, known as LDTI, to improve the accounting for long-duration insurance contracts. The standard introduced several changes:

  • Updated Assumptions for Future Policy BenefitsInsurers must review cash flow assumptions for traditional and limited-payment contracts at least annually and update them when needed, rather than keeping the original assumptions unchanged.
  • Current Discount RatesInsurers remeasure liabilities using an upper-medium-grade fixed-income yield. Changes in liabilities caused by discount rate movements are recorded outside net income.
  • Market Risk BenefitsCertain annuity guarantees must be measured at fair value.
  • Simplified Deferred Acquisition Cost AmortizationDeferred acquisition costs (DAC) are amortized on a constant-level basis.
  • Expanded DisclosuresInsurers must provide more detailed rollforwards and information about assumptions and changes in estimates.

LDTI became effective for most SEC filers for fiscal years beginning after December 15, 2022. Other entities were required to adopt it for fiscal years beginning after December 15, 2024.

Why the Admin System Became a Reporting System

Under LDTI, insurers group contracts into cohorts, typically based on issue year and product type, to measure liabilities. These calculations depend on policy-level information, including issue dates, premiums, benefits, riders, status changes, and actual experience. As experience develops and assumptions change, insurers may also need to update the net premium ratio for each cohort.

Much of this information comes from the policy administration system. Issue dates, premium payments, benefit changes, lapses, reinstatements, and claims flow from the administration platform into actuarial and financial systems. Any errors or changes in these records can affect cohort assignments, net premium ratios, and reported disclosures.

Consider a few examples:

  • Changing how a policy's issue date is stored can place it in the wrong cohort.
  • Missing historical premium data can affect actual-to-expected comparisons.
  • Inconsistent product or rider codes can lead to incorrect cohort groupings.
  • Differences in transaction posting dates can shift financial results between quarters.

The administration system is, therefore, part of the financial reporting control environment. Auditors may examine how data is extracted, validated, and reconciled. A system migration can consequently require additional control testing and documentation, extending the work beyond the operations team.

Why Operations-Led Migrations Put the Close at Risk

Traditional policy administration conversions focus on maintaining service continuity. The new system must bill premiums, collect payments, process claims, and display accurate policy information to employees and customers. These requirements remain important, but they do not cover everything insurers need under LDTI.

When operations leads a migration without sufficient input from finance and actuarial teams, projects may:

  • Map data fields around servicing requirements while overlooking accounting and actuarial needs.
  • Convert current policy values without transferring the full transaction history.
  • Change product or plan codes to simplify system configuration.
  • Test servicing functions without checking cohort assignments and financial reporting outputs.
  • Schedule cutover without considering quarter-end and year-end close activities.

These decisions can break the connection between converted data and previously reported results. Once the new system goes live, correcting the problems may require manual adjustments, revised disclosures, or additional parallel runs. These activities can delay the close and increase the cost of the migration.

Scoping Life Insurance Policy Administration System Projects with Finance as Co-Owner

A successful conversion brings operations, IT, finance, and actuarial teams into project planning from the start. Each team has a different responsibility, but all must agree on how the new system will support policy servicing and financial reporting.

The following steps can help:

  • Define Reporting Requirements EarlyIdentify every data element used in LDTI calculations and disclosures, including the historical records required for reporting.
  • Agree on Data OwnershipEstablish which system serves as the source of record for each data element used in financial reporting.
  • Preserve Cohort IntegrityEnsure that issue dates, product codes, and other attributes that determine cohort assignments remain consistent during conversion. Document and obtain approval for any necessary changes.
  • Convert Historical Data, Not Just BalancesTransfer the premium, benefit, and policy status history needed for experience studies and net premium ratio updates.
  • Plan Around the Close CalendarSchedule cutover and parallel runs to avoid conflicts with quarter-end and year-end reporting.
  • Test Reporting OutputsRun LDTI calculations on the converted data and reconcile the results with the existing system before cutover.
  • Update Controls and DocumentationRecord changes to data flows, validation procedures, and controls so that auditors can review the new environment.

Involving finance and actuarial teams early may extend the planning phase, but it helps identify reporting problems before they affect the close or require costly fixes after migration.

What Life Insurance Policy Administration Systems Software Should Provide

Modern life insurance policy administration systems software needs to support financial reporting as well as day-to-day policy servicing. The right capabilities can help insurers maintain reliable data and reduce the effort required to prepare LDTI disclosures.

Key capabilities include:

  • Complete Transaction HistoryRecords of premium payments, benefit changes, and policy status updates, with both effective dates and processing dates.
  • Consistent Product and Cohort AttributesControls that prevent product, plan, and issue attributes from changing without an approved process.
  • Standardized Reporting ExtractsReliable data feeds to actuarial and financial systems, supported by reconciliation totals.
  • Audit TrailsRecords showing who changed data, when the change occurred, and why it was made.
  • Effective-Dated ChangesSupport for backdated corrections, with a clear record of their impact.
  • Data Quality ControlsValidation rules that identify errors before they reach actuarial calculations and financial reports.

These features help insurers trace reported results back to the underlying policy records. They also make it easier to investigate differences between the old and new systems.

Data Gaps Common in Legacy Blocks

Older insurance portfolios often contain data issues that become more visible when insurers prepare for LDTI reporting or migrate to a new platform. Common problems include:

  • Missing Issue HistoryOriginal policy issue dates may have been replaced with conversion dates during earlier migrations.
  • Incomplete Premium RecordsThe system may contain summary balances but lack the transaction history needed for experience analysis.
  • Inconsistent Product CodingThe same product may appear under several plan codes because of mergers, acquisitions, or earlier system changes.
  • Unclear Rider RecordsRider details may be stored in notes or generic fields instead of structured coverage records.
  • Gaps in Policy Status HistoryLapses, reinstatements, and paid-up changes may lack clear effective dates.
  • Manual WorkaroundsActuarial teams may depend on spreadsheets outside the administration system to fill gaps in the data.

Each issue requires a clear decision. The insurer can reconstruct missing information from source records, apply a documented assumption approved by actuarial and audit teams, or accept a known limitation. These decisions should be made before conversion testing begins. Waiting until parallel runs can leave the team with little time to resolve problems before the planned cutover.

Riders, Reinsurance, and Related Data

LDTI calculations may depend on information beyond the base policy record. Riders can have separate benefits and assumptions, while reinsurance arrangements affect reported results and require consistent mapping between direct and ceded records. Policy loans, dividends, and paid-up additions on participating policies can also affect projected cash flows.

During modernization, insurers should identify these related data sets and determine which system will maintain each one after conversion. If reinsurance administration operates on a separate platform, the migration must preserve the identifiers that connect ceded records to the corresponding policies in the new system.

Without these links, the insurer may struggle to reconcile policy-level information across administration, actuarial, and financial systems.

Staffing the Project

Finance and actuarial teams need enough time to participate throughout the conversion. Assigning people to the project without accounting for their regular reporting responsibilities can create delays, particularly around quarter-end.

Many insurers assign a senior actuary and a financial reporting lead to the project for its full duration. Their schedules should allow time for key decisions and testing during close periods.

Operations and IT can continue to manage implementation and delivery. However, changes affecting financial reporting data should require approval from the designated finance and actuarial leads. External implementation partners should also have experience with both policy administration systems and LDTI reporting. This helps teams resolve differences in terminology and requirements without placing the entire coordination burden on the insurer.

Life and Annuity Software and Market Risk Benefits

Annuity portfolios introduce additional data requirements. Market risk benefits, including certain guaranteed minimum benefits on variable and indexed annuities, require policy-level information about guarantees, account values, and fund allocations to support fair value measurement.

Life insurance software should maintain these records accurately and make them available to valuation systems. During a conversion, insurers need to confirm that the relevant data remains complete, correctly mapped, and accessible for reporting.

Missing or inconsistent guarantee details can affect valuation work and create additional reconciliation tasks. Testing these records before cutover helps reduce the risk of reporting problems later.

Using Modernization to Improve Reporting

A policy administration modernization project can do more than protect existing reporting processes. It can also address data quality problems and reduce the manual work involved in preparing financial results.

Insurers can use modernization to:

  • Reduce manual adjustments by correcting data quality issues at their source.
  • Automate reconciliations between administration, actuarial, and general ledger systems.
  • Speed up the quarterly close with faster and more reliable data feeds.
  • Improve experience studies by making historical policy data more complete and consistent.

These improvements depend on how the new system and its data flows are designed. Finance and actuarial teams need to help define the requirements rather than evaluate the results only after implementation. When reporting needs are built into the design, the new platform has a better chance of shortening the close instead of simply reproducing the existing process.

AI may also help insurers modernize legacy systems. McKinsey has discussed how agentic AI can capture knowledge embedded in legacy insurance platforms and coordinate modernization tasks under human supervision. For life insurers, these capabilities could assist with data mapping and code analysis. Finance and actuarial specialists would still need to review and validate the results, particularly when changes could affect financial reporting.

Testing That Protects the Close

Testing for an LDTI-aware conversion should include:

  • Data ReconciliationPolicy counts, face amounts, premiums, and reserves by cohort, before and after conversion.
  • Cohort ValidationConfirming that each policy remains in the same cohort.
  • Calculation Parallel RunsRunning LDTI calculations on both old and new data and comparing results.
  • Disclosure TestingConfirming that rollforwards and other disclosures can be produced from converted data.
  • Control TestingVerifying new data flows and controls with internal audit.

Parallel runs across at least one quarter-end are often worth the extra effort.

Governance for Ongoing Operations

After conversion, governance keeps reporting stable:

  • Changes to product codes, cohort attributes, or data feeds require finance and actuarial approval.
  • Data quality dashboards track errors and exceptions affecting reporting.
  • Regular reconciliations compare admin data with actuarial and general ledger results.
  • Release management includes reporting impact assessment for every system change.

Measuring Success

Insurers should measure the results of modernization against both operational and financial reporting objectives. Useful measures include:

  • Cohort ReconciliationThe percentage of policies assigned to the same cohort before and after conversion.
  • Reserve VarianceThe difference between liability results calculated using the old and new data.
  • Close TimelineThe time required to complete the quarterly close before and after modernization.
  • Manual AdjustmentsThe number and value of post-close adjustments caused by policy administration data.
  • Data Quality ExceptionsThe number of errors and exceptions identified by validation rules each month.

Tracking these measures helps the project team determine whether the new system is meeting its reporting requirements and where further improvements are needed.

Conclusion

Modernizing a life insurance policy administration system under LDTI is as much a financial reporting project as an operations initiative. The system supplies policy-level data used for cohort-based liability measurement and quarterly disclosures, so decisions about data mapping, conversion, testing, and cutover can affect reported results.

Finance and actuarial teams should be involved from the start and share responsibility for approving changes that affect reporting. Insurers should also preserve historical records, validate cohort assignments, run parallel calculations, and establish controls that continue after implementation.

Before approving the next migration plan, insurers should ask a straightforward question: Has the controller reviewed and approved the data mapping needed to support LDTI reporting? Answering this early can help prevent avoidable reporting problems and ensure the modernization delivers lasting value.
